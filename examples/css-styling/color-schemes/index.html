Colour schemes

A colour scheme is a rule for picking points on the colour wheel. Each row below is one rule, and the swatches are the colours it produces.

1. Monochromatic

One hue, varied by lightness and saturation. Tints, shades and tones of the same colour. Nothing can clash, but contrast has to come from value.

#05255C #0F3880 #2253A8 #477AD1 #8DB2F2

2. Complementary

Two hues 180° apart on the wheel. Maximum contrast — each colour makes the other look brighter. Let one dominate and keep the other for accents.

#2665D1 #F0A422

3. Analogous

Three hues side by side on the wheel. Harmonious and natural, but easy to make look flat — pick one dominant colour and use the rest as accents.

#2FC0D6 #2F96D6 #2F6CD6 #2F43D6 #452FD6

4. Triadic

Three hues evenly spaced 120° apart. Bold, and better balanced than a complementary pair, but all three at equal weight will fight.

#2769DB #DB2769 #69DB27

5. Split-complementary

One base colour plus the two hues either side of its opposite. Nearly the contrast of a complementary pair, but softer and easier to balance.

#2C6CDB #DB432C #C4DB2C

6. Tetradic

Two complementary pairs — a rectangle on the wheel. Rich, and genuinely hard to balance. Without one clearly dominant colour it reads as noise.

#306FDB #9D30DB #DB9D30 #6FDB30

7. Warm and cool

The wheel cut in half. Warm colours advance and feel active; cool colours recede. Using temperature is a way to build depth without adding hues.

#E61C2C #E65F1C #E6A21C #E6CB1C #2CCDDB #2C84DB #2C2CDB #752CDB

Things to try

Change one hue number in the hsl() form and watch which other swatches would have to move with it.

A scheme decides the hues. It does not decide the contrast — that is value, and it is the part that decides whether your text is readable.
