position: fixed

fixed pins a box to the viewport instead of the page. The document scrolls underneath it; the box does not move. A parent with position: relative makes no difference at all.

1 · The bar at the top

top: 0; left: 0; width: 100% keeps the bar across the window. Because it is out of the flow, the page needs padding-top: 60px — that is the bar's height — or the first line of content hides underneath it.

2 · The badge in the corner

The round link in the bottom-right corner is fixed too. Scroll to the end of this page: it has not moved by a single pixel.

3 · Scroll and check

The anchor is the viewport, never the page: the bar looks the same whether the document is 500 pixels tall or 50 000. That is what a header, a cookie bar or a back-to-top button needs — and it is also why a fixed element can hide content, because it takes no room in the layout at all.

Compare it with absolute: an absolute box is measured from a positioned ancestor and scrolls away with that ancestor. A fixed box is measured from the window, which has no scroll position of its own.

There is a legacy trap worth knowing. An ancestor with transform, filter or will-change becomes the containing block for its fixed children, and they start behaving like absolute boxes. If a fixed bar suddenly scrolls away, look for one of those on the way up the tree.

5 · The trade-off

Use it sparingly. Every fixed box covers part of the page at every scroll position, so on a small screen a header plus a footer bar can leave very little room for the content itself, and on a phone the on-screen keyboard can cover a fixed bar entirely.

sticky is often the better answer: it is in the flow, it stops being special once its container has gone, and it never takes space away from the content it belongs to.
